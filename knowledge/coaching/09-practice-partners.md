# Chapter 9: Who You Practice With Matters As Much As How

## 9.1 The ceiling of solo improvement

Everything covered so far — the learning curve, the bias audit, the feedback loop, tilt management, deliberate practice, role awareness — can technically be done alone. In practice, almost none of the players who reach the top tables of any card game got there entirely alone, and the reason is structural rather than sentimental: a single player's blind spots are, by definition, invisible to that player. You cannot debias yourself perfectly through sheer introspection, because the entire mechanism of a blind spot is that it doesn't feel like a gap from the inside. It feels like certainty.

This is the actual function of a good testing group, playtest partner, or competitive circle, and it is worth being precise about what "good" means here, because a low-quality version of this same structure can be actively harmful. A weak testing group mostly agrees with itself, mostly plays the matchups it already understands, and mostly reinforces whatever the group's strongest personality already believes. A strong testing group does close to the opposite: it actively disagrees, plays the matchups nobody wants to play, and treats a confidently stated opinion as something to be tested rather than deferred to.

## 9.2 What to actually look for in other players

Three traits are worth prioritizing when choosing who to test and review games with, and none of them is simply "skill level," because a higher-rated player who agrees with everything you already think adds far less than a moderately skilled player who consistently sees the board differently than you do.

The first is honest disagreement. A testing partner who tells you a line was good because they don't want the conversation to be awkward is worse than no testing partner at all, because they give you the comfort of feedback without any of its actual value. The second is a genuinely different perspective, which is exactly why studying and discussing with players of other card games is not just a nice-to-have but a real source of edge, as covered in Chapter 5 — someone whose entire intuition was built inside a different game's specific patterns will see your board state through an unfamiliar lens, and that unfamiliarity is precisely what surfaces blind spots a same-game training partner would share with you. The third is a willingness to actually change their mind in front of you when the evidence calls for it, because this models the exact behavior covered in Chapter 8 — treating beliefs as hypotheses — and makes it socially easier for you to do the same instead of digging in to defend a position out of pride.

## 9.3 Teaching as a form of practice

There is also a less obvious benefit to spending time helping newer players improve, one that experienced competitors across many skill domains, from chess to poker to esports, have independently rediscovered: explaining a concept clearly enough for someone else to understand it is one of the more demanding tests of whether you actually understand it yourself, rather than merely executing it from pattern memory. A player who has only ever played intuitively, without being able to explain their reasoning, often discovers real gaps in their own model the first time they try to teach a genuinely curious newer player why a particular line is correct. This is not a detour from your own improvement, it is a direct continuation of the stage-three work described in Chapter 1 — converting intuition into explicit, examinable reasoning — applied under the added pressure of an audience who will ask "why" until the explanation either holds up or breaks down.
