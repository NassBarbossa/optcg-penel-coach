# Chapter 2: The Biases That Are Quietly Sabotaging Your Self-Assessment

If the learning curve chapter established that your win-loss record is a noisy signal, this chapter is about something more uncomfortable: your own brain is actively distorting how you read that signal, in predictable and well-documented directions. This is not a character flaw specific to you. It is a set of cognitive shortcuts that every human brain uses, and that the poker, chess, and Magic communities have spent decades writing about because they kept noticing the same self-deceptions in their best players.

## 2.1 Outcome bias: judging the decision by the result

This is the big one, and almost certainly the single most expensive bias in any competitive card game. Outcome bias is the tendency to judge the quality of a decision by how it turned out, rather than by the quality of the reasoning at the time the decision was made, given the information available then.

A concrete example: you are at four life with a clear lethal swing available if your opponent doesn't have a specific trick in hand. Statistically they have it maybe one time in five. You attack. They have it. You lose. Outcome bias tells you "that was a bad attack." It was not a bad attack — it was a good decision that lost the one-in-five roll. If you make that same attack every time the math says to, you win it four times out of five over a long run. Punishing yourself, or worse, "learning" not to make that attack next time, actively makes you a worse player, because you are training yourself based on noise rather than signal.

This bias runs in the opposite direction too, and that direction is in some ways more dangerous because it feels good. You make a greedy, low-percentage play, it works, and you conclude the play was correct, because a win is a win. Poker players have a phrase for this: "results-oriented thinking." Every strong poker player learns to separate "I won the hand" from "I played the hand well," because over a large enough sample, players who consistently make +EV (positive expected value) decisions win, and players who get lucky on -EV decisions eventually stop getting lucky. The same separation has to happen in your head as a card game player. A loss with a correct decision is not a failure to learn from. A win with an incorrect decision is not a success to repeat.

## 2.2 Confirmation bias: only noticing the games that prove your point

Once you have decided that a deck is "good against aggro" or that a card is "underrated," confirmation bias makes you notice and remember the games that support that belief far more vividly than the games that contradict it. This is why deck-building debates in every card game community are so resistant to evidence — both sides are often pattern-matching off a self-selected, biased subset of their own play history. The cure is not "trust your gut less" in some vague sense, it is structural: write down results in a way that doesn't let you cherry-pick later, a topic the feedback loop chapter covers directly.

## 2.3 Hindsight bias: "I knew that was wrong"

After a loss, it is extremely easy to look back at the critical turn and think "I knew that was a mistake when I made it." Often you did not, in the moment, with the information and time pressure you actually had. Hindsight bias retroactively inflates your sense that the error was obvious, which feels like self-awareness but is actually a distortion — and a damaging one, because it teaches you the wrong lesson. The useful question is never "does this look wrong now, knowing the result," it is "was this the best decision available given exactly what I knew and could calculate at that moment, under the actual time constraints of the game." Sometimes the honest answer is that it was a perfectly reasonable decision that you simply did not have the information to get right, and the lesson is not "I should have known," it's "I need a way to gather that information earlier," which is a completely different and more useful lesson.

## 2.4 The illusion of control

Card games combine genuine decision-making with real randomness — draw order, opponent's hand, matchups in a tournament pairing. The illusion of control is the tendency to overestimate how much of an outcome was within your control versus determined by chance. Players under this bias tend to overrate the impact of their own play (both in the positive direction after wins, and the self-blaming direction after losses caused by bad luck) and underrate the genuine role of variance. This sounds like it contradicts the advice to take responsibility for your decisions, but it doesn't — it is about correctly partitioning outcomes into the part you controlled (your decision quality) and the part you didn't (the random elements), rather than collapsing everything into "I am responsible for this result" or "this was just luck," both of which are oversimplifications.

## 2.5 The sunk cost fallacy at the deckbuilding table

You have spent three weeks tuning a deck around a specific card or strategy. It is not working. The sunk cost fallacy is the pull to keep playing it, or keep trying to make the underperforming piece work, because of the time already invested, rather than evaluating the deck fresh based only on its current and future performance. The hours already spent are gone regardless of what you do next; they are not a reason to continue. This shows up constantly in deckbuilding and far less obviously in in-game decisions too — for instance, continuing to develop a board state you have already over-invested in, instead of cutting losses, purely because of what you already put into it.

## 2.6 Self-serving attribution bias

This is the broad pattern underneath several of the above: the tendency to attribute your wins to skill and your losses to luck, bad draws, or your opponent getting "there." It is psychologically comfortable and almost universal, and it is also the single biggest reason weaker players plateau for years without improving — they have built an internal narrative in which they are never actually the cause of a loss, which means there is never anything specific to fix. Every community has a version of this player: in Magic it's the player who is always "mana screwed," in Yu-Gi-Oh it's the player whose opponent always "had the out," in One Piece TCG it's the player whose opponent always "had the counter." Sometimes that's true! Often it isn't, and the inability to tell the difference is the actual skill gap.

## 2.7 Anchoring on your initial read of the board

A less discussed but equally costly bias is anchoring: the tendency for your first read of a game state, formed in the first few seconds of seeing a new board, to disproportionately shape every subsequent decision in that game, even after new information arrives that should update it substantially. A player who decides early in a match "this opponent is playing the aggressive version of their deck" will keep filtering every later piece of evidence through that initial frame, sometimes long after the opponent's actual line of play has made clear they're doing something different. The fix is not to distrust first impressions entirely — early reads are often genuinely useful — but to treat them explicitly as provisional, and to notice when you are explaining away contradicting evidence rather than updating on it.

## 2.8 Recency bias and the trap of the last big result

Recency bias is the tendency to overweight your most recent experiences relative to the broader pattern of your results. A single dramatic win at a major event can convince a player their deck, or a specific risky line within it, is excellent, even if a more complete look at their results over the preceding months tells a less flattering story. The same distortion runs in the other direction after a single bad loss, especially a public one at an important event, which can trigger an overcorrection away from a genuinely good strategy simply because the most recent data point happened to be vivid and painful. The structured logging covered in the next chapter is, among other things, a direct countermeasure to this bias, because a written record spanning months carries far more evidential weight than whatever happened most recently and most memorably.

## 2.9 Why naming these isn't enough

Knowing about a bias intellectually does very little to protect you from it in the moment — this is one of the more sobering findings in the broader psychology of judgment and decision-making, going back to Daniel Kahneman's work. You cannot simply "try harder not to be biased" and expect it to work, because these biases operate below the level of conscious effort. What actually works is structural: building habits and review processes that route around the bias rather than relying on willpower to overcome it in the moment. That is the entire subject of the next chapter.
